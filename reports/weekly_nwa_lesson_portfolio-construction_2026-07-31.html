Portfolio Construction: Diversification vs. Concentration

July 31, 2026 · Topic: portfolio-construction

Angel investing has a brutal, non-negotiable math problem: most of your companies will return zero, and a tiny handful will return everything. This is the power law, and it dictates the single most important structural decision you make — not which companies you back, but how many and how you size each check. Get the portfolio shape wrong and even good picking can't save you. The counterintuitive truth is that at the earliest stage, diversification is your primary risk-management tool, not concentration. A concentrated angel portfolio of three or four "high-conviction" bets is not a portfolio — it's a lottery ticket with extra steps, because your ability to predict which seed company becomes the fund-returner is, honestly, close to random. You need enough "shots on goal" that your winners have a statistical chance of showing up at all. The consensus lands around 20–30+ positions for an angel who wants a real probability of catching an outlier.

But here's where it gets subtle, and where this week's news makes the lesson urgent: diversification is about uncorrelated bets, not just many bets. In the first half of 2026, over 70% of venture dollars flowed into AI, and even a firm as sophisticated as a16z has been described as running "concentration disguised as diversification" — eight AI positions across three layers still all depend on one macro thesis: that enterprise AI spend keeps compounding. If model margins compress and applications commoditize, spreading bets across a dozen AI labs won't help; they'd all derate together. That's the trap. Owning twenty startups that would all die in the same macro scenario is one bet wearing a portfolio costume. Real diversification means deliberately holding theses that don't share a single point of failure — different sectors, business models, and reasons-to-win — so the thing that kills half your book leaves the other half standing.

Here's the coaching insight business school won't give you: the concentration decision isn't made at entry — it's made through your reserves. The seasoned move is to enter broadly and thinly (many small first checks to buy your shots on goal), then concentrate your capital into the winners as they prove themselves through follow-on rounds. Amateurs spend all their capital on initial checks and have nothing left to defend their best companies. Pros hold back 30–50% of the fund as reserves and deploy it with increasing conviction into the 10–20% of the portfolio that's actually working — buying more of the winner at Series A/B where the risk has dropped but the upside is still enormous. You diversify to find the outlier, then you concentrate to own more of it. That two-phase shape — broad entry, concentrated doubling-down — is how the best angels turn the power law from a threat into their entire edge.

Key Terms

Shots on Goal — The number of independent investments in a portfolio. Because early-stage returns follow a power law, the probability of holding at least one outsized winner rises sharply with position count; below ~20 positions, an angel is statistically likely to miss the fund-returner entirely, no matter how good their picking feels.

Reserve Ratio — The share of total investable capital held back (typically 30–50%) for follow-on investments rather than spent on initial checks. Reserves are how you concentrate into proven winners at later rounds — the mechanism that lets you diversify at entry and concentrate on conviction later.

Real-World Example

Union Square Ventures' 2004 fund is the classic illustration: a relatively small, deliberately diversified early-stage portfolio where a couple of positions — Twitter and Zynga among them — returned the entire fund many times over, while plenty of others returned little or nothing. The fund worked because it had enough shots on goal to catch outliers, not because USV knew in advance which companies those would be. The contrast case is the concentrated angel who put most of their capital into three or four seed companies in the same hot sector: when that sector's thesis wobbled, the whole book moved together and there was no uncorrelated winner to carry it.

This week's data — 70%+ of venture dollars chasing a single AI thesis — is that concentration risk playing out at the whole-industry scale, and it's precisely why "how many uncorrelated bets do I actually hold?" is the question to ask before writing the next check.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Lay out every angel position you hold (or are seriously considering) on one page and tag each with its core thesis — not its sector label, but the one macro bet it needs to be right for it to win (e.g., "enterprise AI spend keeps compounding," "physical automation reaches cost parity," "regulatory tailwind for X"). Now count: how many genuinely different theses are you exposed to, and what single scenario would take down the most positions at once? If more than half your book rides one macro bet, your next check should be chosen at least partly to break that correlation — and decide today what reserve ratio you'll hold back to double down on whichever names prove themselves.
