Fundable vs. Investable

September 4, 2026 · Topic: fundable-vs-investable

Every deal that reaches the table eventually gets evaluated on two completely different axes that get treated as one blended gut feeling. Fundability is about the pitch: does this team, market, and moment tell a story investors already believe — one legible enough to underwrite quickly off a deck and a few reference calls, even with almost no hard data? Investability is about the business: strip the narrative away entirely — do the actual economics (unit economics, retention, defensibility, capital efficiency) make this something worth owning equity in, story or no story? The two overlap often enough that conflating them feels harmless. It isn't. A company can be wildly fundable and quietly uninvestable, or genuinely investable and structurally unfundable for venture money — and each mistake costs an angel in a different way.

At pre-seed and seed, you have almost no numbers to lean on, so fundability is doing nearly all the work in the room — the founder's storytelling, the category's heat, the social proof of who else is circling the round. That is exactly the moment the split matters most, because the story is loudest precisely when the evidence is thinnest. Run the same lens in the other direction and you'll also see durable, profitable, well-run companies pass through NWA's pipeline — real revenue, real margins, a founder who can run a P&L — that are genuinely investable but structurally unfundable for a venture check, because the market ceiling can't produce the 50–100x outcome a fund model needs. Treating "worth funding" and "worth backing as venture capital" as the same question is one of the most common, and most expensive, mistakes an angel makes.

Here's the coaching insight a business-school case study won't give you: score fundability and investability as two independent variables, and never let a high score on one quietly stand in for the other. A great story riding weak unit economics is a trap dressed as opportunity — the room's excitement is the tell, not the evidence, and the more oversubscribed a round feels, the harder you should be pushing on the numbers, not less. A great business with a small market or no venture-scale story isn't a bad investment; it's the wrong instrument, and it may deserve your capital through a note with a modest cap, a revenue-share structure, or simply your introduction to a search-fund buyer rather than a venture check it can never repay at fund-return math. The discipline isn't choosing one lens over the other — it's refusing to let either one answer for the other.

Key Terms

Fundability (Narrative-Market Fit) — The degree to which a startup's story — team pedigree, market heat, traction narrative, momentum and social proof — matches what investors are currently primed to believe, making it easy to underwrite quickly even on thin data. High fundability accelerates a raise; it says nothing about whether the underlying business is sound.

Investability (The Unit-Economics Case) — Whether a business, judged strictly on its own numbers — retention, margins, capital efficiency, defensibility, path to durable profit — is genuinely worth owning equity in, independent of how well the story pitches. A company can be highly investable and still fail to raise venture money if its ceiling can't clear the fund's return bar.

Real-World Example

WeWork is the textbook fundable-but-not-investable case: a magnetic founder, a category riding real momentum, marquee investors chasing the round, and a story ("we elevate the world's consciousness") that made the fundraising itself feel like validation. Strip the narrative away and the business was a commercial real-estate arbitrage — long lease liabilities against short sublease terms, thin structural margins — that no amount of "tech company" framing could fix. It was extraordinarily fundable, and it turned out not to be investable at nearly any price. Run the comparison the other way and you get the profitable regional services and vertical-SaaS businesses that search funds and private-equity buyers compete over every year: genuinely investable, cash-generative, defensible — and routinely passed on by VCs, not because the business is bad, but because a realistic exit can't move a large fund's return math. Same two questions; opposite answers, in both directions.

Jamie's Action Prompt

On the next deal NWA reviews, score it twice on two separate index cards before the group discussion — one for fundability (team, market heat, story, momentum: would this raise easily right now?) and one for investability (unit economics, retention, defensibility: would you still want to own this if the story vanished tomorrow?). Bring both scores to the table instead of one blended gut call, and flag out loud any deal where the two diverge sharply — that gap is exactly the signal this lens is built to catch.
