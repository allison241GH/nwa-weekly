Anti-Dilution Provisions and Their Effects

September 4, 2026 · Topic: anti-dilution-provisions

Anti-dilution provisions are the clauses in a preferred-stock deal that adjust an investor's conversion price downward if the company later sells shares at a lower price than the investor paid — a "down round." In plain language: if you buy in at a $10M pre-money and the next round prices the company at $6M, an anti-dilution provision quietly hands you extra shares to partly compensate for the fact that you overpaid relative to the new reality. It is not about the ordinary dilution of new money coming in (that happens to everyone and is healthy); it is specifically a price-protection mechanic that kicks in only when a later round is cheaper than yours. There are two flavors that matter, and the gap between them is enormous.

Why this matters for an angel or seed investor specifically: you almost never set these terms — the lead and the company's counsel do — but you inherit them, and they can silently gut your position. The dangerous one is full ratchet, which reprices your entire stake as if you'd paid the new, lower price, transferring a brutal amount of ownership from founders and common holders to the protected investor. The market-standard one is broad-based weighted-average, which adjusts only in proportion to how big and how cheap the down round actually was. In the two-tier market of 2026 — where Carta-sourced data this week showed roughly a fifth of 2025 rounds were down rounds — the difference between these two clauses is the difference between a founder who is demoralized but still motivated and a founder whose cap table is so broken they walk. As an early investor, a founder-crushing ratchet upstream of you is your problem too, because it destroys the incentive of the people you're betting on.

The coaching insight business school won't give you: anti-dilution is a symptom, not a cure. Seasoned investors know that the real protection against a down round is never the ratchet — it's making sure the company raises enough runway to reach a milestone that justifies a markup before the money runs out. A punishing anti-dilution clause you "won" in the term sheet is worth nothing if it fires, because by the time it fires everyone at the table has already lost. The move that actually protects your seed check is diligence on the plan to the next round: how much runway, what milestone, and what that milestone is worth if hit. Terms are the parachute; runway is not jumping out of the plane in the first place.

Key Terms

Full-Ratchet Anti-Dilution — The aggressive form: if the company issues any new shares below the investor's original price, the investor's conversion price is reset all the way down to that new lower price, regardless of how few shares were sold. It maximizes protection for the holder and maximally dilutes founders and common — rare in healthy deals, and a red flag when a later investor demands it.

Broad-Based Weighted-Average Anti-Dilution — The market-standard form: the conversion price is adjusted using a formula that weighs both the price of the down round and the number of new shares issued relative to the total shares outstanding (the "broad-based" part includes options and convertibles in that denominator, softening the adjustment). It compensates the investor proportionally rather than punitively.

Real-World Example

The cleanest cautionary tale is Square's 2014 Series E. The company raised at $15.46 per share with a full-ratchet-style IPO-price protection that guaranteed those investors a minimum return at IPO — so when Square went public in 2015 at $9, well below the private mark, the ratchet forced the company to issue millions of extra shares to make those late investors whole, diluting everyone who came before them at the worst possible moment. It became the textbook lesson that structure taken to raise a headline valuation is a loan against your own cap table.

Contrast that with how most 2026 down rounds are being papered: broad-based weighted-average is the default, precisely because the ecosystem learned that founder-crushing ratchets create the death spiral they were supposed to prevent.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Pull the term sheet or charter of one NWA portfolio company this week and find the anti-dilution clause. Confirm two things: (1) is it weighted-average or ratchet, and (2) is it "broad-based" (options and convertibles in the denominator) or "narrow-based." Then ask the founder the more important question the clause is really about — "if you had to raise your next round today, at what price, and how many months of runway do you have to avoid that?" The clause tells you how much a down round would hurt; the runway answer tells you how likely it is.
