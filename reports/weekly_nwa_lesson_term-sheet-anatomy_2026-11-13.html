Term Sheet Anatomy — Key Terms and What to Negotiate

November 13, 2026 · Topic: term-sheet-anatomy

A term sheet is a two-to-four page non-binding summary of the deal, and almost everyone reads it wrong. First-time angels fixate on the headline — the valuation — because it's the one number they understand. But the valuation is the price; the term sheet is the structure, and structure is where the real money moves after the exit. A founder who "wins" a high valuation but concedes a 2x participating liquidation preference, full-ratchet anti-dilution, and a stacked board has sold far more of the company than the cap table suggests. Your job as an investor is to read past the number to the economics and control the terms actually encode.

For angels and seed investors specifically, the stakes are asymmetric. You rarely set the terms — you're usually accepting a lead's term sheet or a founder's SAFE — so your leverage is in knowing which terms to care about and which to wave through. Over-negotiating a $50K check signals you don't understand your place in the stack; failing to notice a participating preference or a pay-to-play provision means you get washed out in exactly the mediocre-exit scenarios that make up most of the portfolio. The term sheet sorts the two kinds of terms: economic terms (who gets how much money, and when) and control terms (who decides what). You need to read both columns.

Here's the coaching insight business school won't give you: the terms that matter most are the ones that only bite in the bad outcomes. In a 50x home run, everyone's preferences convert to common and the structure is irrelevant — the power law does the work. The term sheet earns its keep in the 1x–3x "muddle" exits, which are the majority. A 1x non-participating preference is market and fair; a 2x participating preference means the founder (and you, if you're common-adjacent) eats sand in a modest sale while the lead takes the first bite twice. When a founder calls a term "standard," ask standard for whom — and model the $40M exit, not the $4B one. This week's OpenAI round is the macro version of the same lesson: it closed the $11B tranche at $1.25T, below the $1.4–1.5T it first sought — proof that even the most sought-after company negotiates on price and structure, and that accepting a cleaner number beats reaching for a headline you have to engineer.

Key Terms

Liquidation Preference — The guaranteed payout preferred shareholders receive before common (founders, employees) see a dollar in an exit. "1x non-participating" (get your money back or convert to your ownership %, whichever is greater) is the founder-friendly market standard; "participating" means you get your money back and your pro-rata share of the rest — a double-dip that quietly transfers value from founders to investors in smaller exits.

Pro-Rata Rights — Your contractual right (not obligation) to invest in future rounds to maintain your ownership percentage. For angels this is the single most valuable economic term on the sheet, because it's how you keep betting on your winners before they get too expensive — fight to keep it, even when everything else is boilerplate.

Real-World Example

When Sequoia and others backed early-stage companies through the 2021 froth, a wave of founders accepted sky-high valuations that came bundled with structured terms — participating preferences, ratchets, and senior liquidation stacks — to justify the price. As the 2022–2023 reset hit, many of those "high valuation" rounds proved to be structured debt in disguise: at flat or down exits, the preference stack consumed most of the proceeds and the common equity the founders thought they'd protected was worth little.

The clean lesson that filtered back into standard practice: a lower valuation with a 1x non-participating preference is almost always worth more to a founder — and signals a healthier company to the next investor — than a high valuation propped up by structure.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Pull the most recent term sheet or SAFE in your NWA deal flow and build a simple two-column "exit waterfall": at a $30M, $100M, and $500M exit, how much does the preferred stack take off the top before common participates? If you can't answer that in five minutes from the document, that's your signal that the structure — not the valuation — is where your next diligence hour should go.
