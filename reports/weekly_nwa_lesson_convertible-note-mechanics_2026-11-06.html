Convertible Note Mechanics — Discount, Cap, Interest

November 6, 2026 · Topic: convertible-note-mechanics

A convertible note is debt that is designed never to be repaid in cash — it's a loan that converts into equity at the next priced round. Three levers do all the work: the discount (you convert at a percentage below the price the next round's investors pay — typically 15–25%), the valuation cap (a ceiling on the price at which your note converts, no matter how high the next round prices), and the interest rate (accrues and converts into more shares rather than being paid back). The elegant part is that a note lets a company raise money now without having to agree on a valuation now — you defer the hardest negotiation to the priced round, when there's more information and a lead investor to set terms.

Why this matters so much for angels specifically: the cap and the discount are not redundant — they protect you in opposite scenarios, and you convert on whichever is better for you. If the next round prices modestly, the discount is what rewards you for being early. If the company becomes a breakout and the next round prices sky-high, the cap is the only thing standing between you and getting diluted down to a rounding error — your early risk converting at a late-stage price. A note with a juicy discount but no cap looks founder-friendly and generous until the company 10x's before the priced round, at which point you funded the riskiest chapter and captured almost none of the upside. The cap is the single most important number on the page.

Here's what a seasoned investor will tell a first-time angel that business school won't: the headline cap is a valuation in disguise, so treat it like one — then do the share math, not the vibe math. Founders (and over-eager angels) talk about notes as if they're "cheaper" or "simpler" than a priced round, but an uncapped or high-capped note stacked on top of other notes can quietly create a cap table that detonates at conversion — founders discover they sold far more of the company than they thought, and early angels discover they own far less. Before you wire, model the actual conversion: at a plausible next-round price, how many shares does your note become, and what percent do you end up owning? If you can't answer that in a spreadsheet, you don't understand the deal — you're just trusting the word "convertible" to mean "fine."

Key Terms

Valuation Cap — The maximum company valuation at which your note converts into equity, regardless of how high the next priced round values the company. A lower cap means more shares for you at conversion; it is the primary protection for early investors against being diluted by a big up-round.

Conversion Discount — The percentage reduction off the next round's per-share price at which your note converts (e.g., a 20% discount lets you convert at 80% of what new investors pay). It rewards early risk when the next round prices at or below the cap.

Real-World Example

The convertible note's rougher reputation traces to deals where founders raised round after round of uncapped notes, then hit a huge priced round — and only then realized how much ownership had silently converted away, souring relationships right when the company was winning. That pain is exactly why Y Combinator introduced the SAFE in 2013: a simpler instrument that kept the cap-and-discount logic but stripped out the debt mechanics (maturity date, interest, repayment risk) that made notes a trap for both sides. The lesson for an angel is that the instrument matters less than the inputs — a SAFE and a convertible note with the same cap and discount convert to nearly the same ownership; what wrecks returns is a bad cap, a stack of notes nobody modeled, or terms agreed on by feel.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Take the most recent note or SAFE in your NWA pipeline (or the last one you did) and build a one-page conversion model: pick a realistic next-round pre-money, then compute your shares and final ownership two ways — converting on the cap vs. converting on the discount — and confirm you'd take the better of the two. Then stress it: if the company 5x's before the priced round, does your cap still protect you, or did you quietly fund a breakout for almost no ownership? If this week's mega-rounds (fal doubling to $16B in seven months) teach anything, it's that up-rounds can come fast — and an uncapped note is a bet that they won't.
