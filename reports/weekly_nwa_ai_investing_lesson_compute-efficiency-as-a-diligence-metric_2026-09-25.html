Compute Efficiency as a Diligence Metric

September 25, 2026 · Topic: compute-efficiency-as-a-diligence-metric

For most of AI's first act, investors underwrote capability — whose model is smartest — and treated compute as a background cost the founders would "figure out later." That era is ending, and this week made the reason concrete: Anthropic shipped Claude Opus 5.5 at roughly 40% lower run-cost for near-frontier capability, and OpenAI split GPT-6 into cheaper task-tiered variants. When the price of intelligence drops 40% on a single release, the durable question for a startup stops being "how good is your output" and becomes "what does it cost you to produce each unit of that output, and where is that cost heading." Compute efficiency — cost per task, per query, per agent-run — is graduating from an engineering footnote to a core diligence metric, the AI-era equivalent of gross margin.

Why this matters for an angel or seed investor specifically: two AI companies can show identical demos and identical revenue, and one is a great business while the other is a subsidy machine — the difference is invisible unless you ask about inference economics. A startup burning $1.50 in compute to deliver a task it charges $1.00 for has negative gross margin that scales with its own success; every happy customer deepens the loss. In the seed diligence conversation, the tell is whether the founder can answer cost-per-task and how it trends without flinching. Founders who treat inference cost as a first-class metric — instrumenting it, driving it down with caching, smaller fine-tuned models, routing cheap queries to cheap models — are building a business; founders who wave it away as "compute will get cheaper" are betting their unit economics on someone else's roadmap.

Here's the coaching insight a seasoned AI investor would share that the hype cycle obscures: falling model prices cut both ways, and which way depends entirely on positioning. If a startup's product is thin — a light wrapper whose value is mostly the model's — then a 40% price cut mostly flows through to customers (or to competitors who undercut on price), and the startup captures little. But if the startup has a real moat — proprietary data, workflow lock-in, distribution — then falling compute costs drop straight to its gross margin, and it gets more profitable every quarter without raising prices. So compute efficiency isn't just a cost question; it's a moat test in disguise. The right diligence move is to model the company's economics assuming inference gets 10x cheaper: if that scenario crushes them (because their edge was cost arbitrage), pass; if it fattens them (because their edge is elsewhere), you've found operating leverage most investors aren't pricing in yet.

Key Terms

Cost-per-Task (Unit Economics of Inference) — The fully-loaded compute cost to deliver one unit of the product's value (a completed query, document, agent-run), versus the price charged for it. The single most revealing AI-startup metric: it exposes whether growth compounds profit or compounds loss, and its trend line signals whether the team is actually engineering efficiency or hoping model prices bail them out.

Gross-Margin-on-Inference — Revenue minus the direct compute cost of serving it, as a percentage. The AI analog to SaaS gross margin — but far more volatile, because the "COGS" (model API pricing) can drop 40% in a single vendor release, mechanically expanding margin for companies whose value sits above the model and doing little for those whose value is the model.

Real-World Example

This week's Claude Opus 5.5 launch is the live case. Anthropic explicitly positioned it as matching prior-frontier quality at ~40% lower cost ($4/$20 per million tokens). Now run the two-sided test on a portfolio: an application-layer company like a Harvey or a Cognition (both recently priced at foundation-model scale) benefits from that cut — its legal or coding workflows cost less to run overnight, and if its moat is proprietary data and enterprise lock-in, the savings drop to margin. But a generic "chat with your PDF" wrapper competing on price sees the same cut hand its customers a reason to expect lower prices, or hands a competitor room to undercut.

Same 40% price drop, opposite outcomes — and the only way to tell them apart in diligence is to have asked, up front, where the company's value lives relative to the model it rents.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Pick one AI company in the NWA pipeline (or one you're evaluating) and ask its founder a single question this week: "What's your cost-per-task today, and what does it become if model prices fall another 40%?" Then judge the answer type, not just the number — a founder who has the figure at their fingertips and can explain whether a price cut helps or hurts them is running a real business; one who deflects to "compute keeps getting cheaper" is telling you their moat is cost arbitrage, which the next Opus release can erase overnight.
