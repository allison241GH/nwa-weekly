Market Sizing: TAM/SAM/SOM and When They Matter

August 30, 2026 · Topic: market-sizing-tam-sam-som

Market sizing is the exercise of estimating how big the revenue opportunity is, sliced into three nested layers. TAM (Total Addressable Market) is everyone who could ever conceivably buy the product — the whole ocean. SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market) is the slice you can actually reach given your geography, business model, and go-to-market. SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) is what you can realistically capture in the next three to five years given competition and your own execution. The number that matters least is the one founders love most: the giant TAM.

For an angel or seed investor, market sizing is not about the size of the number — it's about the quality of the reasoning behind it. A founder who slaps a $100B TAM on slide 4 with no bottom-up check is telling you they don't understand their own funnel. The fastest way to lose a sharp investor in 2026 is exactly that: a top-down TAM that reads like a Statista summary, with no unit economics underneath. The market has tightened since 2021 — investors are running stricter ideal-customer-profile math and demanding a bottom-up build (customers × price × frequency) before they'll take the top-down story seriously. Data from this cycle even suggests founders who present both top-down and bottom-up close meaningfully faster.

Here's the coaching insight business school won't give you: the size of the market is a filter, not a thesis. A huge TAM never made anyone money — capturing a real, defensible slice of a reachable market did. What a seasoned VC actually listens for is whether the founder can name their first 100 customers by type, price the wedge, and describe the second and third act that expands SAM over time. The best pitches often lead with a deliberately small SOM (a narrow, winnable beachhead) and earn the right to a big TAM by showing a credible path to expand into it. When the market is genuinely nascent, TAM is unknowable anyway — and there, you're underwriting the founder and the wedge, not the spreadsheet.

Key Terms

TAM / SAM / SOM — Total, Serviceable Addressable, and Serviceable Obtainable Market: the whole opportunity, the reachable subset, and the realistically capturable near-term slice. Investors weight SOM and the reasoning behind it far more than a headline TAM.

Bottom-up vs. Top-down sizing — Top-down starts from an industry total and shaves it down ("1% of a $100B market"). Bottom-up builds up from units (number of target customers × price × purchase frequency). Bottom-up is far more credible because it forces the founder to know their actual customer and price point.

Real-World Example

When Uber raised early money, a famous critique pegged its TAM at the size of the existing taxi-and-limo market — a modest, top-down number. That framing badly understated the opportunity, because Uber's low prices and convenience expanded the market (people took rides they'd never have taken in a taxi). The lesson cuts both ways: top-down sizing anchored to today's incumbents can be wildly wrong for genuinely new categories. The best seed investors sized Uber bottom-up — rides per city × price × cities they could realistically enter — and underwrote the market-expanding wedge rather than the static taxi TAM.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Pull the most recent pitch deck in your NWA deal flow and ignore its TAM slide entirely. On one page, rebuild the market bottom-up yourself: who is the first customer type, what will they pay, how often, and how many of them are reachable in 24 months? If you can't answer those four from the deck, that's your first diligence question — and a tell about how well the founder knows their own business.
