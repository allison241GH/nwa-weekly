How VCs Think About Risk at Each Stage

September 19, 2026 · Topic: risk-at-each-stage

Every venture check is a bet against a specific risk, and the single biggest mistake a first-time angel makes is underwriting the wrong one for the stage. A company doesn't carry one undifferentiated blob of "risk" — it carries a stack of them, retired one layer at a time: team risk (can these people build anything?), product risk (can they build this?), market risk (does anyone want it?), go-to-market risk (can they sell it repeatably?), and scale risk (does it work at 100x with the unit economics intact?). At pre-seed you are almost purely buying down team and product risk — there is no market proof to underwrite, so you're paying for conviction in founders and a thesis. By Series A you should be paying for evidence that market risk is largely retired (real product-market fit signals), and by growth stage you're underwriting execution and scale risk, not "will this work" — you're paying for speed and durability, which is why the price is so much higher.

For an angel, the practical stakes are that the risk you're paid to take is the risk that's still live at your entry point. If you invest at seed and the thesis is "great team, unproven market," your return comes from being right about the market before it's obvious. If you invest at a hot Series B where the market is proven, you're no longer being paid for that insight — you're paying a premium because it's retired, and your return now depends entirely on execution and terminal size. Mispricing this is how people lose money in "safe-looking" later rounds: they pay a de-risked price but the company still faces an un-retired risk (usually go-to-market or margin structure) that the valuation assumed away.

Here's the coaching insight business school won't give you: the best returns come from buying down the last remaining piece of a specific risk, not from avoiding risk. Seasoned investors hunt for the company where one clear risk is about to be retired and the market hasn't repriced it yet — the seed team about to ship the thing that proves the market, the Series A about to cross from "founder-led sales" to "repeatable playbook." That's asymmetry: high perceived risk, low actual remaining risk once you've done the work. And the corollary — in a frothy tape like this week's, a lot of capital is paying seed-level prices for the illusion that all risk is retired simply because a name is hot. Your edge is knowing exactly which risk is still standing.

Key Terms

Risk retirement — The process by which a startup systematically eliminates categories of uncertainty (team → product → market → go-to-market → scale) as it matures. Each funding round should, in theory, correspond to retiring one major risk; a valuation step-up without a risk retired is just multiple expansion.

Stage-appropriate valuation — Pricing a deal against the risks still live at that stage rather than the company's ultimate potential. A "cheap" late-stage deal with an un-retired go-to-market risk can be more dangerous than an "expensive" seed with a clear path to retiring market risk.

Real-World Example

Cognition (maker of the Devin coding agent) illustrates the whole stack compressed into months. At its Series D in May 2026 it was valued ~$26B on ~$492M run-rate — investors were still partly underwriting market risk (would developers actually pay for autonomous coding agents?). Four months later, its September round priced it at $48B on ~$900M run-rate: the market risk had visibly retired (revenue nearly doubled, adoption proven), so the price doubled. The lesson cuts both ways — the early Series D investors were paid for taking the still-live market risk, while the new Series E buyers are now underwriting almost pure execution and scale risk at a $48B entry, betting the AI-coding market stays large and Cognition stays a leader. Same company, completely different risk being priced.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Take the most active deal in your NWA pipeline and write one sentence: "The specific risk I am being paid to take here is ______, and the evidence that it's about to retire is ______." If you can't fill in the second blank, you're either too early for the price or being asked to pay a de-risked valuation for a risk that's still live — either way, that's your negotiation.
