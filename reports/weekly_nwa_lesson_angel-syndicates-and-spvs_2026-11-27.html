Angel Syndicates and SPVs

November 27, 2026 · Topic: angel-syndicates-and-spvs

A syndicate is a lead angel pulling a group of backers into a single deal, and the SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle) is the legal wrapper that makes it work: one LLC is formed to hold one investment, every participant buys a membership interest in that LLC, and the cap table of the startup sees exactly one line — the SPV — instead of forty individual names. That's the entire magic. A founder closing a hot round does not want forty signatures, forty wires, and forty people with information rights; they want one clean entry. The SPV lets a syndicate lead deliver that while still spreading the economics across a crowd.

For angels specifically, SPVs do two things nothing else does. First, they let you write a $10K or $25K check into a deal whose minimum would otherwise be $100K+ — you ride on the lead's allocation. Second, they turn access into a product: the best syndicate leads get into oversubscribed rounds precisely because they can fill a $500K slot in 48 hours with soft-circled capital, and they trade that speed for allocation. The flip side is that you are now a passive LP in someone else's deal — you did not negotiate the terms, you may not have done the diligence, and you are paying for the privilege.

The coaching insight business school skips: in a syndicate, you are underwriting the lead at least as much as the company. A great company inside an SPV run by a lead who takes 20% carry, charges setup fees, and can't get you clean K-1s on time is a worse investment than a merely-good company run by a disciplined lead. Before you click "invest" on any syndicate, you should be able to answer: what is this lead's real track record (not their best logo), how much of their own money is in this specific SPV, and what exactly are the fees and carry? If the lead won't show you their skin in the game, that's your answer.

Key Terms

SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle) — A single-purpose legal entity (usually an LLC) formed to make one investment. Participants own interests in the SPV; the SPV owns the shares. It appears as one line on the startup's cap table regardless of how many underlying investors there are.

Carried Interest ("carry") — The share of profits the syndicate lead keeps as compensation, typically 10–20%, paid only after investors get their capital back. A 20% carry means on a 10x winner, the lead takes 20% of the gains above your principal — material, and worth modeling before you commit.

Real-World Example

This week's Sierra round is a perfect illustration of why SPVs exist at the top of the market. Bret Taylor's Sierra closed $1.75B at a $30B valuation, led by Greenoaks and Lightspeed — allocation that is effectively closed to individual angels. When a deal is that oversubscribed, the only way a smaller investor touches it is through an SPV: a connected lead secures a slice of a late-stage allocation (or secondary) and syndicates it to a crowd at, say, a $25K minimum. That access is real — but so is the stacking of fees and carry on top of an already-rich entry price, and the dilution of your information rights down to a quarterly update. The lesson AngelList institutionalized a decade ago still holds: SPVs democratize access, not terms.

Jamie's Action Prompt

Pick one syndicate platform you already have access to (AngelList, or an NWA-affiliated SPV) and pull up the last deal you were offered. Write down three numbers before you'd ever invest: the total fee drag (setup + management), the carry percentage, and the lead's own dollar commitment to that specific SPV. If you can't find the third number, email the lead and ask — and notice how they respond. That single question separates the leads worth following from the ones selling access to deals they don't personally believe in.
