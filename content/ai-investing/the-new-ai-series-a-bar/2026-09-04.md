The "Series A bar" is the informal but very real threshold a startup has to clear to raise an institutional Series A — and in the AI era it has moved so far, so fast, that founders and even some investors are still quoting numbers from a market that no longer exists. Two years ago, a strong SaaS company could raise a Series A on **$1–3M of ARR** and a good story. As of 2026, the data circulating this week pegs the median Series A near **$15M at a ~$79M post-money**, with the revenue expectation jumped to **$5–10M ARR** — and a hard split underneath the median: AI and healthcare rounds routinely clear $50M while fintech and consumer sit at $8–14M. The bar didn't just rise; it *bifurcated*. "Series A" now means two completely different things depending on whether the AI is the product or just a feature.

Why this matters for an angel or seed investor specifically: the Series A bar is the gate your seed check has to survive. You are not underwriting the seed round in isolation — you're underwriting whether this company can plausibly reach a *now much higher* milestone before its runway ends, because if it can't clear the new A bar, your seed position gets crushed in a down round, a bridge, or a shutdown. This is where the AI era gets genuinely dangerous for early investors: AI tailwinds make it *easier* to get to a few million in revenue (the tools are cheap, the demos are magic) but *harder* to defend it, so a wave of companies will sprint to $3M ARR — the old bar — and then stall out below the new $5–10M gate, in the exact valley where seed investors lose money. The easy early traction is a siren, not a signal.

The coaching insight a seasoned AI investor would share but no framework teaches: at the new bar, **efficiency is the underwriting, not growth.** When capital was cheap and AI didn't exist, Series A investors bought growth and forgave burn. Now, with agentic tools letting a five-person team do what thirty used to, the A-round question has flipped to "how much revenue per employee, and how little did it cost to get here?" A company hitting $6M ARR with 8 people and a burn multiple under 2x is a fundable AI-era Series A; a company hitting the same $6M with 40 people and heavy inference costs is a much harder sell, because it has demonstrated *that AI didn't give it the leverage the whole thesis depends on*. As a seed investor, the most predictive question you can ask a founder is no longer "how fast are you growing" — it's "at your next raise, what will your revenue-per-employee be, and does that prove AI is actually a moat for you or just a cost?"

## Key Terms

**The Series A Bar** — The set of metrics (chiefly ARR, growth rate, and increasingly *efficiency*) a startup must hit to raise an institutional Series A. It is a moving, market-set threshold — currently ~$5–10M ARR for AI companies in 2026, up sharply from the $1–3M of a few years ago — and clearing it is the milestone most seed rounds are implicitly betting on.

**ARR Efficiency (Revenue per Employee)** — A measure of how much recurring revenue a company generates per head, used as a proxy for whether AI leverage is real. In the AI era, investors increasingly treat high revenue-per-employee (and a low burn multiple — net burn ÷ net new ARR) as evidence of a durable advantage, and a bloated headcount at the same revenue as evidence the "AI efficiency" story is hollow.

## Real-World Example

This week's fresh seed activity shows the pattern in miniature: **Metal** raised a $4.5M seed (a16z Speedrun, Y Combinator) building AI fundraising software with a tiny team, alongside a slate of small-team, verticalized rounds (Axle AI's $17.5M Series A in insurtech; a €5M seed in data engineering). The common thread the reporting keeps flagging is that capital is flowing to AI applied to a *specific workflow* with a *small team* — precisely the revenue-per-employee profile the new Series A bar rewards. Contrast that with the broader 2026 benchmark data: the median Series A jumped to ~$79M post on $5–10M ARR *because* the winners are demonstrating this efficiency, dragging the bar up for everyone — including the 40-person companies at $4M ARR who will find the new gate closed.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Take one company in NWA's current pipeline or portfolio and compute two numbers before your next partner discussion: its current ARR-per-employee, and the ARR it will need at its next raise to clear a 2026 AI-era Series A ($5–10M, adjusted for its sector). Then ask the founder directly how few *additional* people they need to get there. If the answer implies revenue-per-employee is *rising* toward the raise, AI is a real moat for them; if it implies they need to double headcount to double revenue, you've found the crack the new bar will expose — better to see it now than at the A.
