Due diligence used to be a rate-limiter. Reading a data room, cross-checking financials, scanning a market, calling references — that took weeks, and the friction was a feature: it forced patience and filtered out the impulse buys. AI has collapsed that. Long-context models now ingest an entire pitch deck, a data room, and a set of financial models in a single pass; purpose-built tools like Wokelo and the newer VC-diligence stack generate structured reports — team background, market dynamics, competitive positioning, financial benchmarks — in hours instead of weeks. The reported result is a **~35% average cut in evaluation time**, up to **60% on document-heavy diligence**, and **~85% of private-capital dealmakers now using AI daily**. Diligence at machine speed is here.

Why this matters for an angel or seed investor specifically: when the *mechanical* part of diligence goes to near-zero cost, it stops being a source of edge. If you and every other investor can generate the same competitive teardown in an afternoon, that teardown is now table stakes — it protects you from obvious mistakes but it doesn't *win* you anything. Worse, machine-speed diligence compresses the whole timeline: founders run tighter processes, rounds fill faster, and the pressure to "keep up" pushes investors toward faster *yes/no* decisions on more deals. Speed without a corresponding increase in judgment is just a faster way to be wrong at scale.

The coaching insight a seasoned investor would share: **when diligence gets cheap, the value migrates from gathering information to exercising judgment on it.** The AI can tell you the market is $40B and the team has the right pedigree; it cannot tell you whether *this* founder will still be obsessed in year four, whether the "moat" survives the next model release, or whether the thing that looks like a red flag is actually the reason this company will work. Use the machines to erase the grunt work — reference synthesis, financial reconstruction, competitor maps — and reinvest every hour you save into the two things that don't automate: talking to founders and customers, and forming a contrarian view. The investors who win the AI-diligence era won't be the ones with the best tools; everyone will have those. They'll be the ones who use the time the tools give back to think harder than the crowd.

## Key Terms

**Automated diligence** — Using AI (long-context models, purpose-built diligence platforms) to perform the information-gathering and synthesis steps of due diligence — document review, financial reconstruction, market sizing, competitive mapping — in hours rather than weeks.

**Judgment premium** — The rising share of an investor's edge that comes from interpretation and conviction rather than information access, in a world where the information-gathering step is commoditized by AI. As diligence gets cheaper, the judgment premium is where returns are actually earned.

## Real-World Example

This week made the tension concrete. YC's Summer 2026 Demo Day put **234 companies** in front of ~1,500 investors on Sept 10 — a firehose no human could diligence the old way in the days a hot round stays open. An investor using AI can now spin up a structured teardown on all 234 overnight; the parallel reality is that *everyone else can too*, so the teardown decides nothing. The same week, Affinity's 2026 VC-tooling survey pegged **~85% of dealmakers using AI daily**, and platforms like Wokelo advertised full diligence reports in hours. Put those together and the edge is obvious in the negative: the winning investor at a 234-company Demo Day isn't the one who *read* the most decks — the machine read them all — it's the one who used the machine to clear the noise and then spent their scarce human hours on the three founders worth a real conversation.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Take one live NWA deal and split your diligence explicitly into two columns: "what a model can do for me" (market sizing, competitor map, financial reconstruction, reference synthesis) and "what only I can do" (founder conviction, moat-durability call, the contrarian bet). Run the first column through Claude this week, then spend the time you save entirely on the second. Notice how much of your old process was information-gathering you can now delegate — and whether your actual *judgment* is sharp enough to carry the decision once the busywork is gone.
