An investment memo is the short document you write to *yourself* (and your co-investors) before you wire money, arguing both sides of the deal and landing on a decision. Two to four pages is plenty: what the company does, why now, the market, the team, the deal terms, the three things that have to go right, the three things that could kill it, and your actual recommendation with a number. It is not a pitch deck recap and it is not a sales document — it is the discipline of forcing your gut feeling to survive contact with the written word. If you can't write the memo, you don't understand the deal well enough to fund it.

For angel and seed investors specifically, the memo is the single highest-leverage habit you can build, because at the earliest stage you are investing in *judgment under uncertainty* and the memo is where your judgment gets audited — first by you, later by reality. It does three things at once: it slows you down enough to beat FOMO, it creates a dated written record you can grade yourself against when the outcome lands in five years, and it makes your thinking *transferable* so a syndicate or co-investor can pressure-test it. The investors who compound get better not because they have better gut instincts but because they write down their reasoning and then go back and read it after the exit (or the wipeout).

The coaching insight business school won't give you: **the memo's most valuable section is the one arguing against the deal.** Anyone can write the bull case — the founder already did it for you in the deck. The pros force a "why this fails" section and a pre-mortem, and they *weight their conviction by how well the company survives that section*, not by how exciting the upside is. In a market where $450M Series A rounds are landing on pre-revenue AI teams, the memo is your personal circuit breaker against buying hype at a growth-stage price. Write the kill-case first; if the deal still stands after you've tried to murder it on paper, that's signal.

## Key Terms

**Investment Memo** — A concise written analysis (typically 2–4 pages) an investor writes before committing capital, covering the company, market, team, terms, key risks, and a clear recommendation. Its purpose is decision discipline and a dated record, not persuasion.

**Pre-Mortem** — A technique where you imagine it's three years from now and the investment has failed completely, then write down *why*. Doing this before you invest surfaces risks that optimism hides during live diligence.

## Real-World Example

Sequoia's internal investment memos are semi-legendary — the firm's 1990s memo on a young Google and its memo on Airbnb (which initially framed a skeptical "people will never rent a stranger's couch" risk, then argued why that objection was wrong) are case studies in the genre. The Airbnb memo matters precisely because it *named the biggest reason to pass* and then reasoned through it, rather than ignoring it. That's the move: the strongest memos don't hide the scariest risk, they drag it into the center of the page and decide whether the upside is worth it anyway. Contrast that with the typical 2026 FOMO round, where the "diligence" is a 30-minute call and a wire — no memo, no kill-case, no record to learn from.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Take the most recent deal you looked at for NWA (funded, passed, or still open) and write a one-page memo on it this week — but write the "Why This Fails" section *first*, before the bull case. Then notice: did forcing the kill-case change your conviction, or confirm it? That gap is the exact value the memo adds, and it's a habit worth making non-negotiable before every check.
