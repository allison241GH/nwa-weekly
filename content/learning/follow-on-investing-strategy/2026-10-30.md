Follow-on investing is the decision to put *more* money into a company you've already backed, usually at a later, higher-priced round. It sounds simple — double down on your winners — but it's where most angels quietly destroy returns, because the instinct that feels right is almost always backwards. The concept has two parts: **reserves** (capital you deliberately hold back from your initial allocation to deploy later) and **selection** (which of your portfolio companies actually earn that follow-on check). A disciplined angel decides *both* before the first wire goes out, not in the emotional heat of a hot up-round.

Why it matters so much for angels specifically: your initial check buys a tiny slice, and the power law means one or two companies will drive your entire fund's return. Follow-ons are how you concentrate capital into those few winners *after* you have real signal — traction, a priced round led by a credible investor, a founder who executes. But the trap is that follow-on rounds are most available and most seductive exactly when a company is hot and expensive, and the companies that *need* more money to survive will beg you hardest. The undisciplined angel averages *down* into losers (throwing good money after bad to "save" the investment) and under-funds winners (because the up-round feels "too expensive"). Pre-committed reserves and a cold selection rule are the antidote.

Here's what a seasoned VC will tell a first-time angel that business school won't: **your follow-on decision should be made as if you'd never invested in the company before.** The sunk cost of your first check is irrelevant. Ask only: "At *this* price, with *this* new information, is this one of the best deals I can make today?" If the honest answer is no, don't follow on — even if it's your own portfolio company, even if passing feels like disloyalty. Loyalty is to your returns, not to your ego's need to be right about the first check. And protect your **pro-rata rights** in your initial deal terms, because the single most painful follow-on mistake is wanting to back a breakout winner and being *unable* to, because you never secured the right to.

## Key Terms

**Follow-On Investment** — Additional capital invested into an existing portfolio company in a subsequent financing round, typically to maintain or increase ownership as the company's valuation rises.

**Reserve Ratio** — The share of your total capital-for-this-company (or fund) that you deliberately set aside for follow-ons rather than deploying in the first check. A common professional framing is reserving $1–$2 for follow-ons for every $1 of initial investment into your winners.

## Real-World Example

Union Square Ventures' investment in Coinbase is the canonical lesson. USV didn't just write an early check — it maintained and expanded its position across later rounds as conviction and evidence grew, so that by IPO its ownership (and dollar return) was dramatically larger than the first check alone would have produced. The discipline wasn't "invest early"; it was *recognizing a breakout and having both the reserves and the pro-rata rights to keep buying.* The mirror-image lesson plays out constantly in angel portfolios: investors who spread their reserves evenly across every company (including the stragglers) end up under-weighted in the one name that actually returned the fund — the exact opposite of what the power law rewards.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Pull up your NWA portfolio and, for each company, write a one-line follow-on verdict using the "would I invest today, fresh?" test — *Yes, concentrate* / *Hold, need more signal* / *No, this check is done.* Then check: for every "Yes," do you actually hold the pro-rata rights to act on it?
