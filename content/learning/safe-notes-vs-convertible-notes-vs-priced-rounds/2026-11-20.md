At the earliest stages, you're almost never buying stock — you're buying a *claim on future stock*, and the instrument you use determines how much of the company you actually end up owning. There are three choices. A **SAFE** is a short, standardized agreement (invented by Y Combinator) that gives you the right to convert into equity at the next priced round, usually with a valuation cap and/or a discount, but with no interest, no maturity date, and no debt. A **convertible note** is the older cousin: economically similar but structured as *debt* — it accrues interest and has a maturity date, which gives you theoretical leverage if the company never raises again. A **priced round** is the real thing: you negotiate an actual pre-money valuation, the lawyers paper a full Series Seed or Series A, and you receive shares today at a known price per share.

Here's why the distinction matters for an angel specifically: SAFEs and notes feel founder-friendly and fast — you can close in a day with a two-page document — but they *defer the valuation fight*, and deferred valuations have a way of surprising you. Stack three uncapped SAFEs on top of each other and you can wake up at the priced round owning far less than your check implied, because every SAFE converts at that round's (higher) price. The cap is your only protection, and if you didn't negotiate a tight one, you paid seed risk for Series A ownership. A priced round costs more in legal fees and takes longer, but you know *exactly* what you own the day you wire — there's no conversion math waiting to dilute you.

The coaching insight business school won't give you: **the instrument is a negotiation about who bears valuation risk, not a formality.** Founders prefer SAFEs because they push the valuation conversation into the future, when the company is (presumably) worth more and the founder has more leverage. As the investor, your job is to pull that risk back toward the present — insist on a cap that reflects *today's* reality, and always model your post-conversion ownership on a fully-diluted basis *including every other SAFE in the stack*, not just your own. A seasoned VC does this arithmetic reflexively; a first-time angel signs the clean two-pager and finds out at the Series A.

## Key Terms

**SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)** — A non-debt instrument granting the right to convert your investment into equity at a future priced round, typically subject to a valuation cap and/or discount. No interest, no maturity date; cheap and fast to execute, which is exactly why discipline on the cap matters.

**Valuation Cap** — The maximum company valuation at which your SAFE or note converts into equity, regardless of how high the priced round actually values the company. A lower cap means more shares for your dollar — it is the single most important economic term you negotiate in an uncapped-vs-capped instrument.

## Real-World Example

This week's Fortune data (Nov 19) makes the stakes concrete: U.S. Series A funding is up ~60% year-over-year to ~$42B, yet the seed-to-Series-A graduation rate has collapsed to 15–20%. That combination is a trap for the undisciplined SAFE investor. In a market where most seed companies *never* reach a priced Series A within 24 months, an uncapped SAFE can leave you holding a converting instrument that either never converts (no priced round ever happens) or converts at a punishing cap you didn't fight for. Contrast that with YC's own playbook: YC funds its batch on a standardized post-money SAFE with an explicit cap precisely so the ownership math is knowable from day one — the standardization *is* the discipline.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Pull the last SAFE or note NWA signed and compute, on a fully-diluted basis, what percentage you'll actually own if the company raises its next round at 2x, 3x, and 5x the cap — including every other SAFE in the stack. If the number at 5x makes you wince, you've just learned why the cap, not the discount, is the term that matters.
