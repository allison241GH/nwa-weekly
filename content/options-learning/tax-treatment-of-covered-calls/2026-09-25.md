Here's the part of covered-call selling nobody warns a beginner about: the premium you collect isn't the whole story on your tax bill — *how and when the option closes* changes what you owe, and a poorly chosen strike can quietly cost you the biggest tax break in investing. In plain terms, there are three ways a covered call ends, and each is taxed differently. If the option **expires worthless**, the premium is a **short-term capital gain**, always — no matter how long you held it. If you **buy it back to close**, your gain or loss is short-term, measured as premium received minus what you paid to close. And if the shares get **assigned** (called away), the premium gets *added to your sale proceeds*, and the whole thing is taxed as a gain on the stock — which could be long- or short-term depending on how long you'd held those shares.

Why this matters for a covered-call seller specifically: the strategy is designed to generate steady income, but that income is almost always taxed at your **ordinary (short-term) rate**, not the friendlier long-term capital-gains rate. That's fine in a tax-advantaged account (an IRA, where none of this shows up on your return), but in a **taxable brokerage account** it means covered-call income is working against you at the margin. The bigger trap is subtler: writing calls against a stock you've held for a long time can **suspend or reset the clock** on your long-term holding period, potentially turning a would-be long-term capital gain on the *shares* into a short-term one if you're assigned. On a position sitting on big embedded gains — say QQQ near an all-time high — that's a real, avoidable tax cost hiding inside a routine income trade.

The coaching insight a beginner misses: the IRS carved out a safe harbor called the **qualified covered call**, and staying inside it is mostly about **not writing calls that are too deep in-the-money**. A call that's at-the-money or modestly out-of-the-money, with more than 30 days to expiration, generally *preserves* your stock's holding period; a deep-ITM call can flip you into "non-qualified" territory and freeze the long-term clock. This is one more reason the strike discipline from the Watch — staying a few percent OTM — isn't just about assignment risk; it's also **quietly protecting your tax treatment.** None of this is tax advice — get your specific situation checked by a professional — but knowing the three exit paths and the qualified-covered-call line turns "why did I owe so much?" into a decision you make on purpose.

## Key Terms

**Qualified Covered Call (QCC)** — An IRS-defined category of covered call that is *not* too deep in-the-money and has enough time to expiration. Writing QCCs generally lets you keep your stock's existing holding period intact; writing a *non-qualified* (deep-ITM) call can suspend the holding period, jeopardizing long-term capital-gains treatment on the underlying shares.

**Holding-Period Tacking** — The rule that determines whether your gain is long-term (held >1 year, lower rate) or short-term (ordinary rate). Selling certain covered calls can *pause* the clock on the shares underneath, so a stock you've owned 11 months might not reach long-term status while an unqualified call is open against it.

## Worked Example

Say Jamie owns 100 shares of QQQ bought years ago near $300, now ~$745 — a large embedded long-term gain. He writes a 30-DTE **$765 call (≈2.7% OTM)** for ~$7.50 ($750). Path A: QQQ drifts sideways, the call expires worthless — the $750 is a **short-term gain**, taxed at his ordinary rate, and his QQQ shares are untouched with their long-term clock intact. Path B: QQQ rips past $765 and the shares are assigned — his sale proceeds become $765 + $7.50 = $772.50/share, and because a 2.7%-OTM call comfortably qualifies as a QCC, the big gain on the *shares* stays **long-term**. Now imagine instead he'd written a deep-in-the-money $700 call to grab a fat premium: that non-qualified call could have suspended his holding period and put that entire multi-hundred-dollar-per-share gain at risk of short-term rates. Same income motive, wildly different tax outcome — decided entirely by strike selection.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Before writing this week's QQQ call, note **which account it's in.** If it's the taxable account, do two quick checks: (1) confirm the strike is at- or out-of-the-money (staying inside qualified-covered-call territory to protect the long-term clock on those low-basis shares), and (2) jot the embedded gain per share so you know what a surprise assignment would actually trigger. If the income is being generated in an IRA, none of this applies — which is itself a useful reason to prefer running the covered-call sleeve there. (Not tax advice — confirm specifics with your CPA.)
