Let's start from the beginning, because this is the foundation everything else in the Options Learning curriculum builds on. A **covered call** is one of the most conservative things you can do with stock you already own. You own at least 100 shares of something — say QQQ or Ford — and you *sell* one call option against those shares. Selling that call means you collect cash today (the **premium**) in exchange for a promise: if the stock climbs above an agreed-on price (the **strike**) by the option's expiration date, you'll sell your 100 shares at that strike. That's the whole trade. You get paid now, and in return you cap how much you'll make if the stock rockets. The "covered" part is what makes it safe: because you already own the shares, you can always deliver them if you're called — you're not exposed to unlimited risk the way a naked call seller is. It's the difference between renting out a house you own and promising to sell a house you don't have.

Why does this matter for someone in your seat? Because a covered call turns a stock you're *already holding* into an income-producing asset. If you own 100 shares of QQQ at ~$688 and sell a call 2–3% out-of-the-money for a ~$9–10 premium, you've just collected roughly $900–1,000 in cash for a month of agreeing to sell at a price above where the stock trades today. If QQQ stays flat or drifts, you keep the shares *and* the premium, and you can do it again next month. That premium is also a thin cushion: if the stock dips a little, the cash you collected offsets part of the paper loss. The dual goal — and this is the frame we'll come back to all curriculum long — is **income plus a little downside protection**, in exchange for giving up some of your upside. It is deliberately a singles-and-doubles strategy, not a swing-for-the-fences one.

Here's the coaching insight a beginner almost always misses: **the covered call's real cost isn't cash — it's opportunity.** You can't lose money you didn't already have at risk (you owned the shares anyway), but you *can* be forced to sell your winner right as it takes off, watching it run past your strike while you're stuck with the premium. That's why *which* stock you write against matters enormously. Writing calls on a name you'd happily sell at the strike is smart income. Writing calls on a name you love and never want to give up — right before a catalyst that could re-rate it — is how you accidentally hand away your best position for a few hundred dollars. The trade isn't "free money." It's a considered decision to rent out upside you've judged you probably won't need this month. Respect that, and the covered call becomes a durable income engine; ignore it, and it quietly clips your best compounders.

## Key Terms

**Covered Call** — Selling one call option against 100 shares of stock you already own. You collect a premium now and agree to sell those 100 shares at the strike price if the stock is above it at expiration. "Covered" means the shares you'd owe are already in your account, so the position carries no unlimited risk.

**Premium** — The cash the option buyer pays you, upfront, when you sell the call. It's yours to keep no matter what happens next. The premium is both your income and a small downside cushion — and its size is driven by how far out-of-the-money the strike is, how long until expiration, and how volatile the stock is.

## Worked Example

Say you own 100 shares of Ford at ~$14.63 (this week's price). You sell one 30-day call at the $15.00 strike for a ~$0.35 premium. Since one contract covers 100 shares, you collect **$35 in cash today** (0.35 × 100). Two things can happen at expiration. If Ford stays below $15.00, the call expires worthless, you keep your shares *and* the $35, and your effective cost basis just dropped by $0.35 — you can write another call next month. If Ford closes above $15.00, you're **assigned**: you sell your 100 shares at $15.00, pocketing the $0.37/share of appreciation ($15.00 − $14.63) *plus* the $0.35 premium — about **$72 total, an ~4.9% "if-called" return** in a month — but you no longer own the shares to ride any further gains. Notice the ex-dividend wrinkle we'll cover soon: Ford goes ex-dividend Aug 11, so if that $15 call were ever in-the-money near then, you could be assigned *early* to capture the dividend — which is exactly why we keep the strike out-of-the-money.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Pick one position you already hold — QQQ or Ford — and, without placing a trade, write down the answer to one question: *at what price would I be genuinely happy to sell 100 of these shares this month?* That price is your candidate strike. Then look up (in your broker) what a ~30-day call at that strike pays in premium, and calculate your two outcomes: the income if it expires worthless, and the total "if-called" return if you're assigned. Doing this once — even on paper — turns the covered call from an abstraction into a concrete, repeatable income decision you fully understand before any money is at risk.
