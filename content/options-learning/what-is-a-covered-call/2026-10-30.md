A covered call is the simplest income strategy in options, and it's "covered" because you already own the shares. Here's the whole mechanic in one sentence: **you own 100 shares of a stock, and you sell someone else the right to buy those shares from you at a set price (the strike) by a set date (the expiry), and they pay you cash up front (the premium) for that right.** You keep the premium no matter what. In exchange, you've agreed that *if* the stock climbs above the strike by expiry, you'll sell your shares at that strike — giving up any gains beyond it. You're renting out the upside above a price you'd be happy to sell at anyway, and getting paid rent today.

Why this matters for an income-focused investor: it turns shares that would otherwise just sit there into a cash-generating position. If the stock stays flat or drifts down, the option expires worthless, you keep the premium, and you can do it again next month — a repeatable income stream layered on top of the dividend. The premium also gives you a thin cushion: if the stock falls, the cash you collected offsets a little of the loss. The trade-off is the one thing a beginner must internalize — **you are capping your upside.** In a screaming melt-up, your shares get "called away" at the strike and you miss the rocket. That's the deal: steady income and a small cushion, in exchange for surrendering the home runs. This is exactly why Jamie writes calls on positions like QQQ and Ford rather than on a stock he thinks is about to triple.

The coaching insight a beginner always misses: **a covered call is a selling-your-shares decision in disguise.** Never write a call at a strike you'd be unhappy to actually sell at, because you might be forced to. The right mindset isn't "how much premium can I grab?" — chasing fat premiums means picking strikes close to the current price, which maximizes the chance your shares get taken. The right question is "at what price would I genuinely be content to let these shares go?" — set the strike there, and the premium becomes a bonus for a sale you'd have been fine making. Get that backwards and a covered call feels like a trap; get it right and it feels like getting paid to place a limit order.

## Key Terms

**Covered Call** — Selling a call option against 100 shares you already own. "Covered" means you hold the underlying shares, so if the option is exercised you simply deliver stock you have — no borrowing, no unlimited risk.

**Premium** — The cash the option buyer pays you up front for the right to buy your shares at the strike. It's yours to keep immediately and permanently, whether or not the option is ever exercised.

## Worked Example

Say Jamie owns 100 shares of **Ford (F) at ~$11.95**. He sells one **~Nov 20 call at the $12.50 strike** for roughly **$0.28** in premium — that's **$28** in cash today (one contract = 100 shares). Two things can happen. If Ford stays below $12.50 by expiry, the call expires worthless: Jamie keeps his 100 shares *and* the $28, and the $28 on $1,195 of stock is about a **2.3% return in a month** on top of Ford's ~5% dividend. If Ford instead rallies above $12.50, his shares get called away at $12.50 — he still keeps the $28 premium and pockets the gain from $11.95 up to $12.50, for an "if-called" return of roughly **7%** — a perfectly good outcome, as long as he was genuinely willing to sell Ford at $12.50. (One wrinkle covered in a later lesson: Ford's ~Nov 10 ex-dividend date means an in-the-money call could be assigned *early* so the buyer grabs the dividend — which is why this week's Watch keeps the strike comfortably out-of-the-money.)
