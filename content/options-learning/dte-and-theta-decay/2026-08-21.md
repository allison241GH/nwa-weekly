When you sell a covered call, you're selling *time* — and time is the one thing that reliably runs out. **DTE** simply means "days to expiration": how many days until the option contract expires. **Theta** is the dollar amount an option loses in value each day, all else equal, purely because it has less time left. As the seller, theta works *for* you: every morning the clock ticks, a little more of the premium you collected becomes yours to keep. This is the engine behind covered-call income.

Here's the part beginners miss: theta decay is **not linear**. An option doesn't bleed value evenly across its life — it decays slowly when there's lots of time left and then accelerates sharply in the final few weeks, curving steeply into expiration. That's why a 60-day option only has a little more premium than a 30-day option even though it has twice the life: most of the "juice" is in that last stretch. The practical consequence is that the **30–45 DTE window is the sweet spot** for selling covered calls — you're capturing the fattest part of the decay curve without committing your shares for months or exposing yourself to too many surprises.

The coaching insight: selling far-dated (say, 90+ DTE) calls *feels* smart because the absolute premium is bigger, but you're earning that premium at a lousy *daily rate* and locking up your upside for a long time. Selling very short-dated (7 DTE) calls captures the steepest decay but forces you to trade constantly, rack up assignment risk near every wiggle, and pay more in friction. The seasoned covered-call seller lives in the 30–45 DTE zone and lets theta do the quiet, compounding work — and often *rolls* or closes at ~50% of max profit rather than squeezing the last few dollars out of the final days.

## Key Terms

**DTE (Days to Expiration)** — The number of calendar days remaining until an option contract expires. Covered-call sellers typically target 30–45 DTE to balance premium quality against how long shares are committed.

**Theta** — The rate at which an option loses value per day from the passage of time alone, expressed as a dollar figure (e.g., theta of −$3 means the option loses ~$3/day). For an option *seller*, theta is a tailwind; for a buyer, it's a headwind.

## Worked Example

Look at this week's QQQ Watch. The ~30-DTE $750 call showed an illustrative premium of about **$6.50**, while the ~60-DTE $765 call showed about **$11.00**. Twice the days, but nowhere near twice the premium — roughly $6.50 vs. $11.00. Break it into a *daily* rate: the 30-DTE earns about **$0.22/day** ($6.50 ÷ 30), while the 60-DTE earns only about **$0.18/day** ($11.00 ÷ 60). Same stock, same idea — but the shorter-dated call pays you more *per day* to rent out your shares, and frees them up a month sooner. That gap is theta's non-linear curve in action: the closer to expiration, the faster the decay, and the better the daily economics for the seller.

Notice which one pays you more per day of committed shares — and let that daily rate, not the bigger headline premium, guide which expiration you actually write.
