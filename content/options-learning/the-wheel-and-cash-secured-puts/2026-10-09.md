So far this curriculum has treated covered calls as a one-way habit: you own shares, you rent out their upside, you collect premium. The **Wheel** is the full loop that comes *before* you own the shares — and it starts with the mirror image of a covered call, the **cash-secured put**. Instead of getting paid to agree to *sell* stock you own at a higher price, you get paid to agree to *buy* stock you want at a *lower* price. You pick a stock you'd be happy to own, pick a strike below today's price, set aside the cash to buy 100 shares at that strike, and sell a put. If the stock stays above the strike, the put expires worthless and you keep the premium — pure income. If it drops below, you get "assigned": you buy the shares at the strike you already liked, with the premium lowering your effective cost even further.

The Wheel simply connects the two trades you already know into a cycle. **Step one:** sell cash-secured puts on a stock you want until you get assigned and own 100 shares. **Step two:** now that you own them, sell covered calls against those shares until they get called away. **Step three:** you're back to cash — so you start selling puts again. Round and round the wheel turns, collecting premium at every stage: put premium while you wait to buy, call premium while you wait to sell, plus any dividends in between. For a patient holder of a stock like **Ford**, this is a structured way to get *paid* for the buying and selling you were willing to do anyway.

Here's the insight beginners miss: the Wheel is only "safe" if you are genuinely willing to own the stock, in size, through a real drawdown — because the one scenario it handles badly is a stock that falls hard and *keeps* falling. You'll be assigned at your strike, then watch the shares drop well below it, now sitting on a loss while you grind out call premium to dig back to breakeven. That's why the Wheel belongs on names you'd be a happy long-term owner of at the strike, sized so assignment never forces your hand. Run on a quality underlying you understand, it's a disciplined income engine; run on a speculative name you're only renting, it's a slow way to catch a falling knife. The premium is never free — it's payment for taking the other side of someone's risk.

## Key Terms

**Cash-Secured Put** — Selling a put option while holding enough cash to buy the 100 shares if you're assigned. You collect premium up front in exchange for the obligation to buy the stock at the strike price through expiration. It's the "entry" half of the Wheel: you get paid to set a limit-buy order at a price you like.

**The Wheel** — A repeating income strategy: sell cash-secured puts on a stock you want to own → get assigned the shares → sell covered calls against them → get called away back to cash → repeat. You harvest option premium (and any dividends) at every step of the loop, on a name you're content to own.

## Worked Example

Say you'd be happy owning **Ford (~$12.21)** at $11.50. You sell a 45-day cash-secured put at the **11.50 strike** for ~$0.25 premium, setting aside $1,150 per contract. Two outcomes: if F stays above 11.50, the put expires worthless and you keep the $25 — about a **2.2% return on the cash set aside in 45 days** (~17% annualized) for agreeing to buy a stock you wanted. If F drops below 11.50, you buy 100 shares at $11.50, but the $0.25 premium makes your *effective* cost **$11.25** — cheaper than today's price. Now you own the shares, so you flip to the second half of the Wheel and sell a covered call (exactly like this week's [Covered-Call Watch](https://nwa-weekly.vercel.app/options/watch/2026-10-09)) at, say, the 13 strike — collecting *more* premium while you wait for the shares to be called away, at which point you're back to cash and start the wheel again. **One caution:** mind Ford's **Nov 9 ex-dividend date** when you get to the covered-call leg, so a short call doesn't sit in-the-money into the dividend.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Pick one name you already know you'd want to own more of — Ford is the natural candidate, or QQQ if you want index exposure — and write down the single price you'd be genuinely glad to buy 100 shares at. Then look up the premium on a ~30–45 day cash-secured put at that strike and calculate the return on the cash you'd set aside. That number is what the market will pay you *this week* just to commit to a purchase you were already willing to make — the entry point of the Wheel, with no obligation to actually place the trade until the math convinces you.
