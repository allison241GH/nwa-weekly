When you sell a covered call, the premium lands in your account immediately — but a raw dollar figure ("I collected $6.50") tells you almost nothing until you turn it into a *rate*. There are two rates worth knowing, and they answer two different questions. **Static yield** asks: if the stock goes nowhere and the call expires worthless, what did I earn just for renting out the upside? It's simply premium ÷ share price. **If-called return** asks: if the stock rises past my strike and my shares get sold ("called away"), what's my total return including the gain up to the strike? It's (strike − price + premium) ÷ price.

These two numbers frame the whole trade, because a covered call has exactly two good outcomes and you should be happy with both. If the stock stalls, you keep the shares *and* the premium (static yield). If it rallies through your strike, you sell at a price you already agreed was fine, and you pocket the premium *plus* the appreciation up to the strike (if-called return). Knowing both up front is how you pick a strike you won't regret either way — the mistake beginners make is fixating on the premium alone and ignoring how much upside they just sold.

The insight most beginners miss is **annualizing**. A $6.50 premium on a $716 QQQ position is a 0.91% static yield — which sounds tiny until you realize you earned it in about *30 days*. Annualized, that's roughly 0.91% × (365 ÷ 30) ≈ **11% per year** if you could repeat it every month. Annualizing is what lets you compare a 30-day call against a 60-day call fairly: the longer-dated call pays a bigger dollar premium but usually a *lower* annualized rate, because time decay (theta) accelerates as expiration approaches. That's the real reason the 30–45 day window is the income sweet spot — you're harvesting the steepest part of the decay curve and getting more at-bats per year. Just don't annualize naïvely: it assumes you can keep re-selling at the same premium, which a calm market won't always give you.

## Key Terms

**Static yield** — Premium ÷ current share price. Your return if the stock is flat and the call expires worthless. This is the "rent" you collect for the obligation you took on.

**If-called (or assigned) return** — (Strike − current price + premium) ÷ current price. Your total return if the stock finishes above the strike and your shares are sold. It includes both the premium and the capital gain up to the strike.

**Annualized return** — A period yield scaled to a full year: yield × (365 ÷ days-to-expiration). It's the only apples-to-apples way to compare calls of different lengths, but it assumes repeatability, so treat it as a comparison tool, not a promise.

## Worked Example

Take this week's QQQ Watch line: shares ≈ $716.56, sell the **~Oct 2 (30 DTE) 735 call** for an illustrative **$6.50**. Static yield = 6.50 ÷ 716.56 = **0.91%**; annualized ≈ 0.91% × (365 ÷ 30) ≈ **11%**. If-called return = (735 − 716.56 + 6.50) ÷ 716.56 = 24.94 ÷ 716.56 = **3.48%** in 30 days. Now compare the **60 DTE 745 call** at ~$11.50: static yield 1.60% but annualized only ≈ 1.60% × (365 ÷ 60) ≈ **9.7%** — a *bigger* check, a *smaller* annual rate. That gap is theta doing its work, and it's exactly why the shorter-dated call is the income workhorse while the longer one is really a "keep more upside" trade.
