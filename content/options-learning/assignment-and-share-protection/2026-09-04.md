When you sell a covered call, you are making a promise: if the stock closes above your strike at expiration, you *must* sell your 100 shares at that strike, no matter how high the stock actually went. That forced sale is called **assignment**, and it is the single event every new covered-call seller quietly dreads and often misunderstands. Here's the reframe that matters: assignment is not a failure. It's the deal working as designed. You got paid a premium to agree to sell at a price you chose in advance, and if the stock reaches that price, you sell — pocketing the premium *and* the gain from your cost basis up to the strike. The only thing you "lose" is the upside *above* the strike, which was never yours to keep once you sold the call. Assignment feels like a loss only because we anchor on the price the stock ran to, not the price we agreed to.

The "share protection" half of this lesson is the part beginners skip. A covered call does two jobs at once: it generates income (the premium) and it provides a *thin* layer of downside protection (that same premium cushions the first bit of any drop). But it is genuinely thin — if you collect $8.50 on a $718 QQQ position, you're protected for about 1.2% before you're net underwater on the shares. So covered calls protect your income and your *upside decision*, but they are not a hedge against a real decline. The trap is confusing "I collected premium" with "I'm protected." If your actual worry is a 15% drawdown, a covered call is the wrong tool; a protective put or simply trimming the position is the right one. The covered call's protection is a rain jacket, not a storm shelter.

The coaching insight most beginners miss: **decide how you'd feel about assignment before you sell the call, not after.** The strike you choose *is* the decision about whether you want to keep the shares. If you'd be genuinely unhappy to sell QQQ at $735 — because you want to hold it for the long-term compounding — then don't sell the 735 call for a little extra yield; sell something further OTM, or don't sell at all that month. If you'd be perfectly happy to sell Ford at $15 for a 6% all-in return, then assignment there is a win, not a loss, and you should stop dreading it. The mistake is selling a strike you don't actually want to honor and then scrambling to roll the call up and out at a loss to "save" the shares — which usually just pays to postpone a decision you should have made cleanly up front.

## Key Terms

**Assignment** — Being required to fulfill your obligation as the call seller: delivering your 100 shares at the strike price because the buyer exercised their right to buy them (typically when the call is in-the-money at expiration, or occasionally early, e.g., to capture a dividend). It is the mechanical event that ends the trade when the stock finishes above your strike.

**Cost Basis** — What you originally paid per share. Assignment sells your shares at the strike, so your realized gain is (strike − cost basis + premium collected). Knowing your cost basis matters for two reasons: it tells you your real return on assignment, and — in a taxable account — whether assignment triggers a short- or long-term capital gain you may or may not want this year.

## Worked Example

Say you own 100 shares of QQQ with a cost basis of $640, and this week you sell the 30 DTE / $735 call for ~$8.50 ($850 total). Two outcomes. If QQQ finishes at $760, you're assigned: you sell at $735, keeping the $95/share gain from your basis plus the $8.50 premium — a $10,350 realized gain — and you "miss" only the $25/share above $735. That's the deal working. If QQQ finishes at $730, the call expires worthless, you keep all 100 shares *and* the $850, and you're free to sell another call next month. Notice that in *both* good outcomes you win; the only bad outcome is QQQ falling hard, where your $850 premium cushions just the first ~1.2% — which is exactly why you'd only write this if you were comfortable owning QQQ here in the first place.
