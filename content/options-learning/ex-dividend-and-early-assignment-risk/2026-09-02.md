When you sell a covered call, you sell someone the right to buy your shares at the strike price. American-style options — which is what you're selling on QQQ and F — let that person exercise **any time before expiration**, not just at the end. In practice almost nobody exercises early, because doing so throws away the option's remaining time value. There is exactly one situation where the math flips, and it's the one every covered-call seller needs to have memorized: **the day before a stock or ETF goes ex-dividend.**

Here's why. A dividend is announced with an **ex-dividend date** — own the shares at the close the day before, and you receive the payment; buy on or after that date and you don't. If someone holds a call that is in-the-money, they can exercise it the afternoon before the ex-date, take delivery of your shares, and collect the dividend. They give up whatever time value is left in the option to do it. So the decision is simple arithmetic: **if the remaining extrinsic value in the call is less than the dividend, exercising early is profitable** — and someone will do it. It is not personal, it's not a judgment about you, and it happens automatically at scale. Your shares are gone before the dividend you were counting on ever hits your account.

The coaching insight beginners miss is that this risk is **entirely avoidable and entirely calendar-driven.** You don't need to model extrinsic value or watch a Greek. You need to do two things: (1) know the ex-dividend dates of everything you own, and (2) never let a covered call sit in-the-money going into one. Deep-OTM calls are effectively immune — nobody exercises a call to buy shares above market. The danger zone is narrow and predictable: a call that has drifted ITM in the final days before an ex-date, especially a short-dated one, where the remaining time value has decayed below the dividend amount. If you check moneyness three days before every ex-date you care about, you will essentially never be assigned early by surprise.

## Key Terms

**Ex-Dividend Date** — The first day a share trades *without* the right to the upcoming dividend. To receive the payment, you must own the shares at the close of the prior trading day. The share price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount on the ex-date, which is why the option holder wants to exercise *before* it, not after.

**Early Assignment** — When the buyer of your call exercises before expiration and your shares are called away early. For covered calls this is almost always dividend-driven and almost always happens on the day before an ex-dividend date.

**Extrinsic Value** — The part of an option's price that is *not* intrinsic (strike-to-price) value — essentially time value plus volatility premium. It's what an early exerciser forfeits. When extrinsic value on your short call falls below the upcoming dividend, assume assignment is on the table.

## Worked Example

Take this week's Covered-Call Watch. QQQ is around **$708** and its next distribution goes ex-dividend on **Sep 21, 2026, at $0.69/share** — which sits inside every one of the 30/45/60 DTE windows. Suppose you sold the 30 DTE **725 call** for $8.00. As long as QQQ stays below 725, the call has zero intrinsic value, its entire price is extrinsic, and nobody would burn that to capture $0.69. You are safe.

Now suppose QQQ rallies to $732 by Sep 18. Your 725 call is $7 in the money. If, with three days to the ex-date, the call is trading at $7.35, its extrinsic value is only **$0.35** — less than the $0.69 dividend. A rational holder exercises on Sep 18's close: they pay $725 for shares worth $732 and pocket a $0.69 distribution you were expecting. Your shares are called away, and you never see the dividend. The fix, taken any time before that, is cheap: roll the call up and out to a later expiry and higher strike, or simply buy it back. The expensive version is discovering it in your account on Sep 21.

Contrast F, where the same risk is currently *absent* by calendar luck: its last ex-date was Aug 11 and the next regular one lands ~mid-November, past the Oct 30 expiry. That's why the Watch flags rolling into a November expiry as the one move that re-introduces the risk on Ford.

## Jamie's Action Prompt

Open your calendar and put two entries in it right now: **Sep 18, 2026 — "check QQQ short-call moneyness before Sep 21 ex-div"** and a recurring quarterly reminder three days before each of QQQ's and F's ex-dates. Then, for any covered call you currently hold, do the one-line check: *is the call's current price minus its intrinsic value bigger than the upcoming dividend?* If yes, you're fine. If it's close, you have a decision to make while it's still cheap to make it.
